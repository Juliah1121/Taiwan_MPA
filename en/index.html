Taiwan MPA Effectiveness Dashboard

From “how much is designated” to “how well is it protected”

Four outcome indicators — water quality, marine debris, biodiversity and enforcement — are combined to evaluate how well Taiwan's marine protected areas actually work. Biodiversity combines ecosystem types, reef checks, blue carbon, field records from the Taiwan Biodiversity Network (TBN, including long-term surveys in four national parks) and a fish environmental-DNA pilot. The dashboard also examines how effectiveness relates to MPA classification, protection level, nearby population and fishing pressure, to inform tiered management and resource allocation.

    Marine biodiversity score

    This is the “biodiversity” dimension of the composite assessment above. Five components, each 0–100, are combined as a weighted mean: species records (weight 1; TBN confirmed species + eDNA-only species (MPA stations and dive survey) × 0.5, converted to a 20–100 percentile among MPAs of the same habitat, +5 for one or more IUCN threatened species and +10 for five or more), coral health (1; latest cover × 1.6 + 20, −10 if declining), blue carbon (1; tier A 100, tier B 70), habitat diversity (1; 1 point per main ecosystem, 0.5 per local one, 3 points = 100) and the eDNA pilot (0.5; percentile of taxa per sample at MPA eDNA stations within habitat). A score is given only if at least one real observation exists: species records, coral or eDNA. Click a column heading to sort.

    How to read this

    Reefs are declining, inside MPAs and out. At Fushan (Shanyuan), live coral was about —% in 2008, and hard coral only —% in 2024–25. Kenting's two sites averaged —% in 2023, and Hejie fell to —% by 2025, with a significant shallow-water decline. Dongyuping in South Penghu dropped from —% in 2023 to —% in 2025, although 2023 measured live coral at 6–8 m and 2025 hard coral, so the decline may be overstated. Only Green Island remains good (about —% in 2023).

    Blue carbon is concentrated in a few places, and half of Taiwan's mangroves lie outside MPAs. Dongsha's seagrass holds about — tonnes of carbon. On the main island the largest mangrove stocks are in the Tamsui River (—), Taijiang (—), Puzi River (—) and Beimen (—). Aogu, the Beigang River mouth, the north bank of the Zhuoshui River, Dapeng Bay and the Yuanlin Drainage each have 40–53 ha of mangrove, yet none is among the 71 MPAs; the largest seagrass bed outside Dongsha, Zhenhai Bay in Penghu (— ha), is unprotected too. On mudflat wetlands such as Xiangshan, mangroves are an invasive expansion that squeezes fiddler-crab and waterbird habitat, so high blue carbon does not mean high conservation value; the rating treats it as just one component.

    Adding field records changed the ranking most where long-term surveys exist. Dongsha (—) and Taijiang (—) have many confirmed records, threatened species and blue carbon, and score highest; Dongsha, South Penghu, Beimen, Qigu Salt Pan and the dolphin habitat, which had no eDNA, now have scores. Conversely, small east-coast reserves with a single eDNA sample (e.g. Xiaohu, Yiwan) dropped, because eDNA now carries half weight and there are no field records to back it up. Coral cover at Fushan (—) and Shitiping (—) has recently fallen to poor, and coral health still pulls their scores down.

    Dive-survey eDNA fills gaps on the outer islands and Green Island. In OCA’s “Survey of Important Marine Ecosystems around Taiwan”, 67 dive eDNA stations (2021–2023) lie inside or within 1 km of 22 MPAs. This gives Maoyu (—), the Wang-an green turtle nesting habitat (—) and Green Island Haishenping (—) a score for the first time, and raises Mianhua & Huaping Islets from 38 to —. These three sites still have fewer than 10 confirmed species, so their scores rest mostly on eDNA and should be treated as provisional.

    Long-term surveys in four national parks. Dongsha (— species) and South Penghu (—) have the most marine fish, with lists about 85% complete, and the most similar assemblages (Jaccard —); South Penghu records — grouper species, the most of the four. Kenting has only —, because its database records are mostly terrestrial and stream surveys (sika deer, land crabs, stream fish), with fewer than 4 marine fish per survey; Kenting's 7 eDNA stations add — fish not recorded by the long-term surveys (awaiting field confirmation), for a combined list of — species. Taijiang is dominated by crustaceans (— species, the most of the four) and has — exotic fish. These records are already part of the nationwide TBN records in the species-record component; survey methods and effort vary greatly between years, so they are not used to judge trends.

    Limitations. The species-record component reflects what is “known so far”: well-surveyed and larger MPAs naturally have more records (area vs biodiversity score ρ = —), while 200 m coastal strips often have no records that can be placed inside them. MPA boundaries mainly follow the Ocean Affairs Council's 2017 layer; Yeliu and Dong'ao are approximate, and Sanxianjiao and Yiwu have no boundary. Reef checks are carried out by volunteers, and 2008 values are read from figures. Blue carbon uses national mean carbon densities. Ecosystem types are assigned manually.

    How to read this

    Legal category does not tell you how well an MPA performs. None of the five dimensions differs significantly between categories (composite Kruskal-Wallis p = —, biodiversity p = —). National parks have the highest biodiversity scores (mean about 84) and the highest composite mean, but there are only four; fisheries resource conservation areas range from 38 to 81.

    Clusters are almost unrelated to categories. Every cluster mixes several categories, and the 31 fisheries areas are spread across all four; the association between category and cluster is Cramér's V = —, permutation p = —, adjusted Rand index = — — no better than chance.

    What really separates MPAs is the type of problem they face. “Clean but unmanaged” sites are small reserves on the east coast and Hengchun (Fengbin, Xiaoma, Xiaogang, Xiaohu, Shuilian, Checheng) and eastern estuaries (Lanyang, Wuweigang, Hualien): the sea is in good shape, but there are almost no patrol or enforcement records. “Managed but debris-laden” sites are concentrated in west-coast estuarine wetlands and the outer islands of Penghu, Matsu and Kinmen, together with South Penghu, the Northeast Coast and the dolphin habitat: patrols and enforcement exist, but litter cancels out the gains. “Poor water, weak management” now covers only Gaomei, Sanxianjiao and Su'ao; water quality at Sanxianjiao and Su'ao rests on a few 2023 samples, so read it with caution. With field records added, biodiversity-rich wetlands such as Taijiang, Beimen and Qigu Salt Pan moved into “balanced performers”; with dive eDNA added, Beinan River Estuary also moved into “balanced performers”, while the Shengang mud shrimp area moved into “managed but debris-laden”.

    What this means for management. Resources and strategies should be tiered by effectiveness type, not applied uniformly by legal basis: unmanaged clusters first need patrols and monitoring, debris-laden clusters need litter clean-up combined with recovery of fishing and aquaculture waste, and the poor-water cluster needs cross-agency control of land-based pollution.

    Methods and limitations. Two or three clusters give higher silhouette scores (—) but merge “balanced performers” and “managed but debris-laden” into one large group, so four clusters are kept (—); agreement with k-means is ARI = —, and — when redone with only the — sites that have all four dimensions. The new biodiversity score blurs the cluster boundaries further (silhouette about 0.2), so the clusters should be read as types rather than sharp classes. Dongsha (only biodiversity and enforcement), Maoyu, Gaoshan and Fanzaishi have too little data and are excluded.

    How to read this

    Overall effectiveness is only weakly related to classification. For type, legal basis and IUCN category, ε² for the composite score is close to 0. Area (ρ = —) and highest protection level (ρ = —) have uncorrected p slightly below 0.05, driven mainly by the new biodiversity score (see below); years since establishment ρ = —. In other words, protection “on paper” and what can be seen in the water and in enforcement are two different things.

    The only consistent signal is in enforcement. MPAs with fully or highly protected zones score higher on enforcement (highest protection level ρ = —, p = —; share of area fully or highly protected ρ = —). This holds after controlling for MPA type and is also seen within Fisheries Act reserves. But it comes mainly from “patrols and detections”, not from “sanctions” — fully protected areas detect many violations but have lower sanction rates (Liuqiu, Dongsha, South Penghu). It looks more like “stricter rules mean more to catch” than effective enforcement.

    Larger MPAs score higher on biodiversity. Area vs biodiversity ρ = — (p = —), the strongest of the 60 associations. This fits the species–area relationship, but also reflects that large MPAs (national parks, the dolphin habitat, large wetlands) are better surveyed; 200 m coastal strips have very few records. Another confounder is that older MPAs have less debris (ρ = —): many old MPAs are east-coast fisheries reserves, where beaches are naturally cleaner, and the effect disappears once the east coast is controlled for. The earlier finding that “more active establishment stages have lower eDNA diversity” (ρ = −0.44 in the old version) disappears with the new score (ρ = —), confirming it was an artefact of single eDNA samples.

    Multiple comparisons. — tests were run and — have p<0.05, where about 3 would be expected by chance; after FDR correction the closest is area vs biodiversity (q = —), and none falls below 0.1. With 71 sites, only associations of |ρ| ≳ 0.33 can be detected, and small groups such as national parks (4) and nature reserves (3) have almost no power, so “no association” should be read as “no evidence of an association”.

    What this means for management. Classification and protection level determine what is allowed; effectiveness depends on whether anyone patrols, whether detected violations are sanctioned, and whether land-based pressures (debris, nutrients) are dealt with. For protection levels to show up in outcomes, the next step is to fill in sanction rates and monitoring site by site, and to bring debris and land-based pollution into management plans.

    How to read this

    Population is linked to debris, but it is not the strongest factor. The denser the nearby population, the lower the debris score (ρ = —), and this holds after controlling for the fishing share (— points per SD). But the east coast is both sparsely populated and faces the Kuroshio with little litter, so the two cannot be separated; once the east coast is controlled for, population is no longer significant. Neither water quality (ρ = —) nor biodiversity (ρ = —) is significantly related to population: water-quality stations are mostly 1–2 km offshore, and site differences are small.

    Fishing and aquaculture pressure explains effectiveness better than population. The higher the share of beach litter from fishing (nets, floats, styrofoam, oyster racks), the lower the debris score (ρ = —). In the multivariable model it is the strongest predictor of the ecological score (— points per SD), while population density in the same model falls to — and is not significant. In other words, the low scores of south-western coastal wetlands, Penghu and Matsu come mainly from fishing and aquaculture waste, not just from more people.

    But there is no evidence that fishing reduces species. The fishing share is not significantly related to the biodiversity score (ρ = —, if anything positive). This does not mean fishing has no effect; current data cannot measure it — species records capture presence only, not abundance or size, and the fishing indicator here is debris composition, not real fishing effort.

    Management input is not linked to ecological outcomes. Patrol hours vs ecological score ρ = —, and — points per SD after controlling for population and fishing, not significant (the significant negative link in the old version, which used eDNA scores, disappears with the new score). Patrol hours correlate positively only with the enforcement score (ρ = —), which is built into the scoring; the positive coefficient on the composite (—) comes from the same circularity. The conclusion stands: effectiveness mainly reflects external pressure and is only weakly linked to management input in its current form.

    Limitations. Population is the total for the township(s) containing or adjoining each MPA, not distance to the coast; figures come from Wikipedia's township list, which does not state a year. Management input covers only patrol staff and hours, not budgets, management plans or monitoring. The data are a one-off cross-section, so they show associations, not causes.
